In 2020, a season played mostly without fans, NFL home teams won 49.8% of their games, the only season from 1999 to 2025 below half. A sighted reader sees that dip in the chart below at a glance. A screen reader user gets it only if the chart says it in text, and needs three more things to use the chart at all: access to the values, a way to explore from the keyboard, and meaning that does not depend on color.

This chart sets no accessibility options.

<Demo id="blog/nfl-home-generated" />

A screen reader meets it as a single `role="img"` with the label "Line chart showing NFL home teams won 59.7% of games in 1999 and 49.8% in 2020 from 1999 to 2025 (27 data points)". The library builds that label from the chart type, your title, the date range and the point count. Treat it as a fallback: it reads awkwardly, and it carries the finding only as far as your title does.

## Text alternatives

Set `a11y.description` and it replaces the generated label. Write the finding there in a sentence or two and leave out the chart type, which tells a listener nothing about the data.

The chart below does this. Its description gives the year synthetic opioids passed heroin, the peak and the drop since.

## Values and keyboard

Next to every live chart the library builds a hidden HTML table of its data, and a screen reader can read it. Its column headers are your field names, so name your fields for a person. The football chart's table opens with `season` and `home_win_pct`. Its numbers are the raw values, unformatted. If you supply your own table, `a11y: { table: false }` turns the generated one off. A visible [data table](/docs/tables/data-table/) beside the chart serves readers who want to look up exact values.

The chart container takes keyboard focus. On line charts the left and right arrow keys step from one x position to the next, the tooltip lists every series at that position, and Escape closes it. In the overdose chart, focus starts at 2015, and two presses of the right arrow reach 2017, where the tooltip reads 28,659 for synthetic opioids and 15,593 for heroin. To announce each step in your own live region, pass `onMarkFocus` when you mount the chart. The [interaction page](/docs/behavior/interaction/) lists the other handlers.

## Color and other cues

The overdose chart gives each line a color, a dash pattern and a name at its right end, so a reader who cannot tell orange from teal can still tell the lines apart. Bars and areas can take `fillPattern: "auto"` to add hatching. The [colorblind palettes post](/blog/colorblind-safe-chart-palettes/) covers choosing the colors themselves.

Pass `dev: true` to `compileChart` while you build and the engine warns when adjacent series fall under the 3:1 contrast minimum for graphics. Two pale blues, `#9ecae1` and `#a6d0e8`, produced "1.07:1 contrast" and a suggested replacement. The [color guide](/docs/style/color/) has the palette options.

## Static SVG has less

`renderStaticSVG` returns an SVG with `role="img"` and the same label, but no hidden table and no keyboard navigation. If you embed static charts, write the finding as a sentence beside the chart and supply the table yourself.

## Check it in the page

Select the chart's `role="img"` element in your browser's inspector and read its `aria-label`. Look for the hidden table beside it, named by its `oc-sr-only` wrapper. Tab to the chart, put the mouse aside and press the arrow keys. Reading the DOM doesn't tell you how a chart sounds, so listen to it in VoiceOver or NVDA before you ship.

## The spec

`a11y.description` holds the alternative text. `theme.colors.categorical` gives each line a light and a dark color, and each one passes 4.5:1 as end-label text on its page. In `encoding`, `color` and `strokeDash` both map the `drug` field, so each line differs in hue and in dash. `endpointLabels` writes the series names at the line ends, and `legend: { show: false }` drops the legend they replace.

[The accessibility docs](/docs/output/accessibility/) map each option to the WCAG success criteria it helps meet.
