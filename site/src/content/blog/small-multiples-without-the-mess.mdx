The Soviet Union launched 108 times in 1982, six times as often as the United States. By 2018, the last year in this data, Russia launched 10 times and trailed both the United States and China. On one chart with four lines that history is hard to follow, because the lines cross: since 1995 all four stay under 40 launches a year and keep swapping places.

<Demo id="blog/launches-one-chart" />

## One panel per series

Small multiples repeat one chart once per series, with the same x axis in every panel. Each line gets a frame to itself, so you read its shape without untangling it from three others.

## Shared or free y scales

Panels can share one y scale, which means a given height stands for the same number in every panel. That keeps level comparisons honest and makes small series look small. In 2025 the US violent crime rate was about 330 per 100,000 residents and the homicide rate was 4.1.

<Demo id="blog/crime-shared" />

On that shared scale homicide is a flat line along the bottom, so its fall from 9.8 per 100,000 in 1991 and its climb from 2018 to 2021 both disappear. Read the 2021 point with some care: the national figures are FBI estimates, and the way police agencies reported to the FBI changed that year.

Giving each panel its own range brings homicide's shape back.

Free scales show whether each series rose or fell and when, and stop showing which is bigger. These panels don't start at zero either, so a one-point change in homicide takes as much height as a change of about 37 points in robbery. Say in the subtitle which choice you made, as both crime charts do.

Use shared scales when the question is which series is larger, and free scales when the question is each series' shape. Neither compares percent change: robbery fell 82% between 1991 and 2025 and aggravated assault 45%, and only an indexed chart or a [log scale](/blog/when-to-use-a-log-scale/) puts those on one axis.

## Ordering and labeling the panels

Panel order is an editorial choice, and alphabetical order is rarely the right one. Sort by the size of each series, by when it peaked, or by the change you want the reader to notice. The launch panels run by total launches from 1957 to 2018, so the biggest program comes first. The crime panels run from the broadest category to the narrowest.

Put the series name above each frame. Use one color in every panel, because position already says which series is which, and leave out the legend.

## When one chart is enough

Two or three lines that stay apart do not need panels. A single [line chart](/docs/change-over-time/line/) lets the reader compare them directly, and splitting them forces an extra look back and forth. Panels start to pay off when lines overlap or cross, and I would split at about four lines.

## The spec

Panels need long data: one row per panel per year, with the date, the series name and the value. `encoding.row` names the series field and stacks one panel per value in a single column, with the years printed under the bottom panel only. `sort: null` keeps the panels in the order of the data rather than alphabetical, and `tickPosition: "gutter"` on the y axis puts each panel's tick labels beside it, where no line can run through them.

A row layout gives each panel its own y range unless told otherwise, so the shared charts set `resolve: { scale: { y: "shared" } }` and the free chart sets `"independent"`. Apart from the title and subtitle, that is the only difference between the two crime specs. Panel axes follow the usual [scale rules](/docs/the-spec/scales/), so a `scale.domain` or `zero: true` on `y` pins every panel if you want them all to start at zero.

Wrapped grids and side-by-side panels, which suit wider screens, are on the [facets page](/docs/composition/facets/).
