Since 1966, US road deaths have fallen 23% and the death rate has fallen 78%. On a chart with two y axes, how different those two drops look depends on the range of each axis, and the author picks both.

The rate is deaths per 100 million vehicle miles traveled. Counts before 1975 come from a different source than later years (state summaries adjusted to 30-day deaths, before NHTSA's own census of fatal crashes began), so the years before 1975 are not strictly comparable with the rest.

<Demo id="blog/dual-axis-zero-based" />

## Zero-based and trimmed axes

With both axes starting at zero, each line falls in proportion to its own change. Deaths lose about a quarter of their height and the rate loses more than three quarters, so this version already shows the rate falling faster.

Trim each axis to the range of its own data and the two drops look closer than they are. Deaths start about 80% of the way up the plot and the rate about 90%, and both reach the floor in 2011, though by then deaths had fallen 36% and the rate 80%. After 2011 deaths climb back to about a third of the plot height while the rate stays near the floor.

<Demo id="blog/dual-axis-trimmed" />

Both charts draw the same 59 rows. Only the tick labels show that the second one was trimmed, and a second axis invites that choice every time it is drawn.

## Separate panels

The first fix is to give each series its own panel, stacked so the years line up. The number of US wildland fires has not climbed since the mid-1980s, averaging about 72,900 a year in 1985-94 and 62,400 in 2015-24, while the average fire grew from 42 acres to 121. The gray segments mark those two decade averages.

The panels start in 1985 because the 1983 and 1984 counts, about 18,000 and 20,000 fires, are a fraction of every later year.

With independent scales, panel heights can't be compared; each panel shows only whether its series rose, fell or held. [How to use small multiples](/blog/small-multiples-without-the-mess/) covers when panels should share one scale and when each should fit its own data.

Indexing has a cost. A base year with an unusual value skews the whole picture, and the real units drop out, so put the raw values in a tooltip or a table when readers need them.

## When a second axis is acceptable

A second axis is defensible when the left series is bars drawn from zero, the right series is a line, and each axis is colored to match its series. If you can't say why each axis has the range it has, use panels.

## The spec

A dual-axis chart is a [`layer` array](/docs/composition/layers/) with `resolve: { scale: { y: "independent" } }`, so each layer gets its own y axis. The two dual-axis charts above differ only in the `scale.domain` and the tick `values` on each `y`. Each layer sets its line color as a `{ light, dark }` pair in `theme.colors.categorical`, so the color holds its contrast on a dark page, and the legend names the lines.

The panel chart is one line mark with `encoding.row` set to the measure field. That stacks the [facet](/docs/composition/facets/) panels in one column, prints the years under the bottom panel only, and gives each panel its own y domain. `sort: null` keeps the panels in data order, and `tickPosition: "gutter"` on the y axis moves the tick labels out of the plot, where a line could run through them. The decade averages are two more series of two rows each.

The indexed chart is one line mark with a `color` field and a fixed `scale.domain` on `y`. The [axes page](/docs/the-spec/axes/) lists the axis options, and the [line chart](/docs/change-over-time/line/) page covers the mark.

The [layers page](/docs/composition/layers/) builds the one dual-axis case that holds up, bars from zero beside a line, with its full spec.
