Argentina's diet comes to about 2,172 kg of CO2 per person a year, the most of the 130 countries in a food-emissions dataset, and beef accounts for 79% of it. Ranking the 20 highest countries means drawing 20 long values that sit close together, which is where a lollipop chart beats bars.

A lollipop thins each bar to a stem and puts a dot at the end. It encodes the same thing as a bar, a value as a distance from zero, with less ink.

## Twenty long bars against twenty stems

As bars, the 20 values run from 1,420 to 2,172 kg and fill most of the plot. The chart becomes a dark block, and the eye has to find each bar's end inside it.

<Demo id="blog/co2-bars" />

As lollipops, each value is a dot at the end of a thin line, and the dots are what the reader compares. The ranking is the same and easier to scan with less fill around it.

## The stem still has to start at zero

A reader judges a lollipop by the length of its stem, the same way they judge a bar. Cut the axis above zero and the lengths stop matching the values. Median weekly earnings run from $738 for adults without a high school diploma to $2,363 for those with a professional degree, a gap of 3.2 times. Start the axis at $600 and the stems measure $138 and $1,763, so the top one is 12.8 times the bottom one.

## Dots without stems

If the point is where values sit rather than how long they are, drop the stems. A dot plot encodes position only, so the same axis from $600 is fair here, because the reader compares where the dots fall and no length stands for a value.

## When plain bars are better

With eight categories and values worth printing, bars win. The earnings chart below puts each dollar figure inside its bar, and the fill makes the gap between a high school diploma ($930) and a bachelor's degree ($1,543) read as a quantity.

<Demo id="blog/education-bars" />

A short list gains nothing from having less ink, and a dot has no room for a printed value. I would use bars for about ten categories or fewer and a lollipop for a longer list.

Bars can also stack and group, and a lollipop can't. Give a lollipop a color field and it becomes a dumbbell instead, which joins the lowest and highest value in each category for any number of series. Once a list is long enough to sit in a dashboard card, a [bar list](/docs/comparison/bar-list/) drops the axis and fits more rows.

## The spec

A lollipop uses the same encoding as a [bar chart](/docs/comparison/bar/): the value on `x` as a quantitative field and the category on `y` as a nominal one. Setting `mark` to `"lollipop"` instead of `"bar"` is the whole change, and `sort` on `y` puts the categories in value order.

The trimmed chart adds `scale: { zero: false, domain: [600, 2400] }` on `x`, with `axis.values` starting at 600 so the cut shows on the axis. Leave that out of real lollipop charts. The dot plot keeps the same axis and sets `mark: { type: "point", trendline: false }`. Lollipop and circle marks always draw stems, and a point mark left with its default trend line fits a regression line through the dots, which means nothing across categories.

Negative values and the dumbbell form are on the [lollipop page](/docs/comparison/lollipop/).
