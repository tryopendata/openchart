A stacked bar chart puts each category's parts end to end, and a grouped bar chart puts them side by side. The same data answers different questions in each form. A stack tells you how big the total is and how the bottom segment moves. A grouped chart tells you how the parts compare with each other.

## What a stack answers

New power plants show the difference. The chart below stacks the capacity of US generators that came online each year, in gigawatts (GW, the maximum output a generator is rated for). The column height is the year's total, which reached 49.8 GW in 2024, and solar sits at the bottom, so its growth from 5.0 GW in 2018 to 31.2 GW in 2024 is easy to follow.

<Demo id="blog/capacity-stacked" />

The data counts only generators that still operate in one snapshot of the EIA's inventory. A plant that opened in 2018 and has since retired is missing, so older years are understated, especially for natural gas and coal.

## What grouping answers

Wind is hard to read in that stack. It sits in the middle, so both its bottom edge and its top edge move from year to year, and the reader has to subtract to see its size. Grouping gives every fuel the same zero baseline.

Now the comparisons are direct. Solar's 31.2 GW in 2024 is 13 times natural gas's 2.4 GW, and batteries (11.1 GW) added more than twice as much as wind (5.2 GW). The total is gone, though, and a reader who wants it has to add four bars. Four fuels across seven years is 28 bars, which is about as many as a grouped chart can hold before the bars get too thin to read.

## Ordering a stack

Only the bottom segment of a stack starts at zero. Every other segment starts where the one below it ends, so comparing it across columns means comparing lengths that start at different heights. Put the series the reader cares about at the bottom, keep the same order in every column, and keep the same color for a series everywhere it appears. Past four or five segments the middle ones get hard to read, and a grouped chart or one panel per series works better.

## When the total is the point

Stacking is the right choice when the total is the story. Counted homelessness in the United States fell to 544,084 people in 2016 and then rose 41% to 767,856 in 2024, and the stack shows that total directly while still splitting it into sheltered and unsheltered people. The top segment is the weak spot: unsheltered people rose from 172,035 in 2016 to 271,350 in 2024, a change that is harder to see on top of the stack than the total is.

If shares matter more than totals, normalize each column with a [100% stacked bar](/docs/part-to-whole/stacked-100/). For many periods, a [stacked area](/docs/change-over-time/stacked-area/) draws the same stack as a continuous shape.

## The spec

Both capacity charts use `mark: "bar"` with the series in `color`. The only difference is the value channel: `stack: "zero"` on `y` stacks the series from a zero baseline and `stack: null` places them side by side. A `scale` on `color` with a `domain` and a `range` pins each fuel to a color, and the domain order sets the stack order, so solar is the bottom segment.

The homelessness chart adds one `text` annotation on the 2021 column, with `x: "2021"` placing it on that category.
