Mortgages make up 70% of what US households owe, and a pie shows that faster than a table, because one slice takes most of the circle. The rest of the debt is harder to show. Car loans, student loans and credit cards sit within $0.4 trillion of each other, and slice angles can't rank them.

## When a pie works

A pie works for two or three parts of a true whole, best when one part dominates. Its slices must add up to 100%, so it is the wrong chart for survey answers where people could pick several options. This one has two slices, mortgages and everything else, and it is drawn as a donut, a pie with a hole that holds the share.

## Ranking the smaller debts

Split the same debt five ways and a pie stops helping. Sorted bars put every value on one baseline, so the order is plain. Student loans lead credit cards by $0.38 trillion, a few degrees of difference on a pie and a clear step between two bars.

Some gaps are too small even for bars. Auto loans ($1.667 trillion) and student loans ($1.664 trillion) differ by about $3 billion, so their bars look the same length, and the printed values carry that comparison. Home equity loans (2.3% of the total) and the New York Fed's own "other" category (3.0%) are merged into one "Other" row.

Sort the bars by value unless the categories have a natural order, such as age bands or months. Bars don't need the parts to add up to a whole, so they also work for values that are not shares. Use them whenever the reader has to compare parts with each other, or when there are more than five or six parts. The [bar chart page](/docs/comparison/bar/) covers the options.

## When a waffle works

A waffle draws 100 squares, one for each percent. It suits a single share you want the reader to feel as a count rather than read as an angle, since counting 66 squares is easier than judging a slice that spans 238 degrees.

In fiscal 2024, payments for individuals took 66 of every 100 federal dollars. That is the Office of Management and Budget's category for money paid to people or spent on their behalf, such as Social Security, Medicare and Medicaid.

<Demo id="blog/outlays-waffle" />

A waffle rounds to whole squares and carries few categories, so it is a poor tool for comparing close values. Keep it to one share and highlight it.

## Shares over time

A pie per year would ask the reader to compare angles across dozens of circles. A 100% stacked area chart scales every year to the same height and shows how the mix shifts. Defense took 49.0% of federal outlays in 1962 and 13.0% in 2024, while payments for individuals went from 27.5% to 66.0%.

## The spec

The donut is `mark: { type: "arc", innerRadius: 0.6 }` with the number on `theta` and the category on `color`, and `centerLabel` prints the share in the hole. A waffle is `mark: "waffle"` with the same two channels, and `highlight` on `color` keeps one category in color and mutes the rest.

The bar chart puts the number on `x` and the category on `y`, with `sort: "x"` to put the longest bar first and `labels.format` to print each value. The area chart sets `stack: "normalize"` on `y` and prints the axis as a percentage with `format: ".0%"`. Each chart lists its colors as `{ light, dark }` pairs in `theme.colors.categorical`, so a value printed in the bar color stays readable on a dark page. The [100% stacked page](/docs/part-to-whole/stacked-100/) and the [waffle page](/docs/part-to-whole/waffle/) cover the stacking and highlight options.

The [pie and donut page](/docs/part-to-whole/pie-donut/) shows how slices under about 3% of a total fold into one "Other" slice.
