Fatal accidents at 56 airlines fell from 122 in 1985-99 to 37 in 2000-14, according to [FiveThirtyEight's airline safety data](https://github.com/fivethirtyeight/data/tree/master/airline-safety). Did every airline improve, or did a few carry the drop? A slope chart answers that in one look: each period gets a vertical edge, each airline gets a line between its two counts, and the steeper the line, the bigger the change.

Every line falls, and Aeroflot's falls furthest. The y axis is gone because the labels carry the numbers: each airline's name and count sit at both ends, so nobody has to match a color to a legend. All lines share one color because the finding is about the group. A merged airline goes by the first name in the source, so "Delta" is the source's Delta / Northwest.

On a phone the left labels go. Both label columns are as wide as the wider one, and two columns of names leave a 390-pixel phone about 40 pixels for the lines. The phone layout puts a y axis on the left for the first-period counts and keeps the names on the right, next to the counts the reader compares.

## Slope chart vs grouped bars

Grouped bars hold the same 22 numbers and rank the airlines within one period better than a slope chart can. To see which airlines improved, though, the reader has to find and compare the bars in pairs.

## Choosing the endpoints

Two points hide everything between them, so the choice of endpoints is part of the argument. The airline data comes in two fixed periods, but Arctic sea ice has been tracked by satellite since 1979, so any two years can be the endpoints. September is each year's low point for ice and March its high. September 1980 has the most ice in the record and September 2012 the least, so this pair shows the steepest drop the data allows.

<Demo id="blog/sea-ice-slope" />

Every September since 2012 has had more ice than 2012 did, and the slope hides that. The full series shows where the two endpoints sit.

<Demo id="blog/sea-ice-line" />

When the path between the points matters, or you have three or more time points, use a [line chart](/docs/change-over-time/line/).

## How many lines fit

Past about a dozen lines, the crossings and labels collide. The airline chart has 11 because it keeps only the airlines with four or more fatal accidents in 1985-99, and the subtitle says so. State the selection rule whenever you show a subset.

Picking airlines for a bad first period also builds in some improvement. Part of any bad record is bad luck, which rarely repeats, so the second count tends to fall even if nothing changed. Statisticians call this regression to the mean. The subtitle gives the total for all 56 airlines, which fell too.

Lines that end at the same value stack their labels, and the engine spaces them apart. Setting `showLeader` adds a short line from a moved label back to its point when the pairing gets unclear.

## The spec

A slope chart is a line chart with two values on the x axis. In the airline spec, `mark` is `"line"` and `encoding.x` is the period field as `ordinal`, which puts one tick at each edge. `axis: false` on `encoding.y` removes the axis and its gridlines.

`color` gives each airline its own line, and `theme.colors.categorical` lists one palette color per airline, all the same teal. Each is a `{ light, dark }` pair, so the labels switch to a lighter teal on a dark page and stay readable. `endpointLabels` with `ends: "both"` and `content: "label value"` prints the name and count at each edge, and `legend: { show: false }` drops the legend. The [endpoint labels page](/docs/framing/endpoint-labels/) lists the other label options.

`overrides.compact` applies below 400 pixels wide. It turns the y axis back on and sets `ends: "end"`, which hands the lines the width the left labels took.

To show the size of each gap rather than its direction, a [range chart](/docs/comparison/range/) draws the two values as a floating bar.

For more than two periods, the [slope and bump page](/docs/change-over-time/slope-bump/) builds a bump chart, which ranks items across many periods.
