A KPI tile has three parts: the current number, a comparison that says whether the number is high or low, and a small line that shows the shape. 

## A sparkline is a line with no axes

A sparkline is a line chart stripped of its title, axes, legend and source line, small enough to sit beside a number. In a box 56 pixels tall, axis labels would take more room than the line, and the reader is not looking for exact values, so the axes go. This one plots the average 30-year fixed mortgage rate each week since January 2021.

<Demo id="blog/kpi-sparkline-bare" />

The shape is a low start, a steep climb and a long plateau. The line carries no scale, so it cannot say that the climb was from 2.65% to above 7%, or that the last point is 7.11%. A reader gets those from the number, which is why a sparkline belongs next to one.

## A number, a comparison and a shape

A number alone does not say whether it is high. The tile below puts the latest rate beside the first rate in the series and the peak, and draws the line under them.

<Demo id="blog/kpi-tile-mortgage" />

The rate of 7.11% is more than two and a half times the January 2021 rate and still below the October 2023 peak.

The peak cell matters most when a series spiked and came back down, as egg prices did.

<Demo id="blog/kpi-tile-eggs" />

The price of a dozen eggs was $2.32 in October 2026, which looks unremarkable until the peak of $6.23 in March 2025 sits beside it. The source has no price for October 2025, and the line bridges that month with a straight segment.

## What every tile must still say

Removing the axes removes the unit, the period and the source, so the tile has to supply them another way.

The unit and the period go in the title, as in "monthly, $". The three tiles here report weekly, monthly and quarterly readings.

The source goes under the tile, as the line under each chart here does.

Each tile also scales its line to its own range, so two lines of the same height can stand for very different changes.

Keep the tiles in one row the same height, and put first the one the reader acts on first. Equal heights make the lines easier to compare for direction.

## Fitting tiles in a grid

The metrics row needs a chart at least 480 pixels wide and, in a test at 300 pixels tall, did not appear, so these tiles are 380 pixels tall. A phone is narrower than 480, so each tile carries a compact-width override that moves the same numbers into its title and subtitle, as the phone view of the mortgage tile does.

For a smaller box, render the label and the number as plain HTML and put a bare sparkline of 36 to 56 pixels beside them.

## The spec

The bare sparkline sets `display: "sparkline"`, which strips everything except the line. Its `mark` is a `line` with a `stroke`, its `x` is a `temporal` field and its `y` is a `quantitative` one.

The tiles use a normal line chart instead, because `display: "sparkline"` would also strip the title and the metrics row. `axis: false` on `x` and `y` removes both axes, and `scale: { zero: false }` lets the line use the full height. `mark.point: "last"` puts a dot on the latest reading.

`metrics` is an array of `label` and `value` cells shown above the plot, and `chrome.title` names the KPI. `overrides.compact.chrome` swaps in the phone title and subtitle. The code that builds the spec writes the label and value strings from the same rows that draw the line.
