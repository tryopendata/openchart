A heatmap is a grid with one category along each side and a colored cell at every crossing. The color is a number, so a table of numbers becomes a picture where dark rows, dark columns and gaps stand out.

## Two categories and one number

Lawful permanent residents, who receive what are called green cards, are counted by country of birth and fiscal year in the [DHS Yearbook of Immigration Statistics](https://ohss.dhs.gov/topics/immigration/yearbook/2023). Here are six countries over ten fiscal years.

<Demo id="blog/green-cards-heatmap" />

Mexico is the darkest row in every year. The pale column in fiscal 2020 runs through every row, so whatever caused the drop hit all six countries at once. That was the first fiscal year of the pandemic, and the total across all countries fell by 31%.

With 60 cells, each cell carries its number, so the reader can check a color against a value. At several hundred cells the labels would crowd each other, and the color and the hover tooltip would have to carry the numbers.

## Choose the color scale

A sequential scale runs from light to dark in one hue, and fits values that only go one way, like counts. A diverging scale runs two hues out from a neutral middle, and fits values that sit above or below a reference point.

The wrong choice hides the pattern. Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii has measured carbon dioxide (CO2) in the air every month since 1958, and NOAA publishes the readings through its [Global Monitoring Laboratory](https://gml.noaa.gov/ccgg/trends/data.html). Each row below is one year and each column one month, on a single sequential scale.

<Demo id="blog/co2-heatmap" />

The rows get darker from 1960 to 2025 and nothing else shows. CO2 climbed by more than 100 parts per million (ppm) between those years, so the trend uses up the whole color range.

Within each year CO2 also rises and falls with the seasons, because plants take up CO2 as they grow in the northern spring and summer and release it in autumn and winter. That swing is about 6 ppm, too small to see against 100. Subtracting each year's own average removes the trend and centers the scale on zero, which makes a diverging scale the right one.

## Order the rows and columns

A heatmap reads by adjacency, so the order of the categories is part of the chart. OpenChart keeps categories in the order they appear in the data, which for months would be wrong if the rows were sorted alphabetically, putting April next to August. Both CO2 charts set the months in calendar order and the years in time order. The green card chart orders countries by size, so the darkest row is at the top.

When no natural order exists, sort the rows by their average value. Similar rows then sit together and the blocks of color are easier to see.

## When another chart is better

Five rows of twelve months is small enough that five lines on a shared month axis would show the seasonal cycle just as well. A heatmap pays off when there are dozens of rows, as in a year-by-state or a person-by-day grid, where lines would pile up. For two numbers per observation, use a [scatter plot](/docs/correlation/scatter/).

## The spec

A heatmap is the `rect` mark. Put one category on `encoding.x` and the other on `encoding.y`, both `nominal`, and a quantitative field on `encoding.color`. A `sort` array on a nominal channel lists the categories in order, from the bottom of the axis up for `y`.

On `color`, `scale.scheme` picks the ramp: `"blue"` is sequential and `"redBlue"` is diverging. `scale.domain: [-4, 4]` centers the neutral color on zero, and `reverse: true` flips `redBlue` so positive values are red. `format` sets how values print in the tooltip. See the [color guide](/docs/style/color/) and the [scales guide](/docs/the-spec/scales/) for the other options.

The cell numbers in the green card chart are two extra [layers](/docs/composition/layers/) of `text` marks drawn over the `rect` layer, which shares its data and its `x` and `y`. One layer filters to cells of 100 thousand or more and draws white text, and the other draws dark text on the rest, so every label stays readable.

The [heatmap page](/docs/correlation/heatmap/) shows fixed color domains and hand-ordered rows.
