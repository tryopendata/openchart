## A title that states the finding

The first title names the topic, and the reader has to work out the point from the columns. The second states what the columns show in a sentence anyone can check: every reading from 1970 on is above zero. Write the title as that sentence, with a number when one carries the point, and keep it to what the data on screen supports.

## A subtitle with the unit

The subtitle says what is measured, in what unit and over what span: degrees Celsius against the 1951–1980 average, one reading per decade. Here it also flags the uneven last step, 2020 to 2025, which a reader would otherwise miss. Put methodology that takes more than a clause in the footer.

## Byline, footer and source

<Demo id="framing/chrome-byline" />

`byline` credits the person or desk that made the chart. `footer` sits at the very bottom and suits notes on method, such as how each column was measured here. `source` names where the data came from; on this site the caption under each figure prints it, so the specs leave `chrome.source` out. In your own charts, set it on every one.

`eyebrow` adds a short label above the title, the kicker some publications use for a section or series name. This site uses none. Each field takes a string, or `{ text, style }` to change its size, weight, color or `maxLines`.

## Fields that matter

| Field | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| `chrome.title` | The headline above the chart. |
| `chrome.subtitle` | What is measured, the unit and the span. |
| `chrome.source` | Data attribution, under the chart. |
| `chrome.byline` | Who made the chart. |
| `chrome.footer` | Notes, at the very bottom. |
| `chrome.eyebrow` | A short label above the title. |
| `chromeLayout` | `"grow"` keeps the plot height fixed and lets a long title make the figure taller. |
